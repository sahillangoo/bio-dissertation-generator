ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is globally acknowledged as one of the major health, developmental & socioeconomic crises of twenty-first century that has renewed interest in plant-derived antimicrobial agents as alternatives to conventional antibiotics. Medicinal plants are promising source of new antibacterial agents because they contain structurally diverse secondary metabolites with broad biological activities. Plant-derived compounds are generally biodegradable and may provide new chemical scaffolds and mechanisms of action for bacterial control. Dipsacus inermis (Himalayan teasel), a perennial herb of the family Caprifoliaceae found across the Himalayan region including Kashmir, has traditionally been used in folk medicine and has shown antimicrobial and phytochemical activity in earlier studies. The present study was undertaken to evaluate the in vitro antibacterial efficacy of an aqueous extract of the aerial parts of D. inermis against three clinically significant Gram-negative pathogenic bacterial strains — Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Klebsiella pneumoniae.

INTRODUCTION
The progression of the antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is recognized worldwide as one of the major health, developmental & socioeconomic crises of twenty-first century. For nearly eight decades, modern medicine has relied on therapeutic regimes anchored by synthetic and semi-synthetic antibiotics to manage infectious diseases, facilitate invasive surgical interventions. The uncontrolled clinical overprescription of antibiotics in clinical practices, have exerted intense evolutionary pressure on pathogenic bacterial populations – accelerated the evolutionary proliferation of resistant bacterial lineages.
According to comprehensive global epidemiological surveys, bacterial AMR was directly linked to approximately 4.95 million deaths globally in 2019 with nearly 1.27 million deaths directly attributable to resistant pathogens. In the absence of immediate development of new treatments, annual mortality attributable to AMR is projected to rise to 1.91 million by the year 2050, concentrated heavily in developing and resource constrained economies.
Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as one of the most pressing global public health challenges of the twenty-first century, undermining the effectiveness of conventional antibiotics and complicating the management of common bacterial infections (Antimicrobial Resistance Collaborators, 2022). Gram-negative bacteria, in particular, are of major clinical concern because of their intrinsic outer-membrane permeability barrier and their capacity to acquire multiple resistance determinants through plasmids, integrons, and efflux pumps. Among these, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Klebsiella pneumoniae are recognised as leading causes of community- and hospital-acquired infections, including urinary tract infections, wound and burn infections, pneumonia, and bloodstream infections, and all three are ranked as the primary contributors of the greatest global burden of bacterial antimicrobial resistance (Antimicrobial Resistance Collaborators, 2022).
Natural products, particularly those derived from medicinal plants, have historically served as an important reservoir for the discovery of antimicrobial compounds (Cowan, 1999). Plant extracts and phytochemicals — including alkaloids, flavonoids, tannins, terpenoids, and phenolic compounds — have been reported to exert antibacterial activity through diverse mechanisms such as disruption of bacterial cell membranes, inhibition of efflux pumps, interference with nucleic acid synthesis, and inhibition of bacterial enzymes (Jubair et al., 2021). Given the slowing pipeline of novel synthetic antibiotics, screening of traditionally used medicinal plants for antibacterial activity remains a valuable strategy for identifying new leads and for validating traditional therapeutic claims through modern scientific methods.
Dipsacus inermis (commonly known as the Himalayan teasel or Chinese teasel) is a perennial herb belonging to the family Caprifoliaceae, genus Dipsacus, which comprises approximately 20 species distributed across China, Europe, tropical Africa, and East Asia, including the Himalayan region (Skała & Szopa, 2023). The plant is easily recognised by its solitary, globular flower heads borne on long, leafless, channelled stalks, with flowering occurring between June and September. D. inermis and related Dipsacus species have long been used in traditional Chinese and folk medicine to manage a range of ailments, including inflammatory conditions, Lyme disease, fibromyalgia, and bacterial infections (Zhao & Shi, 2011; Hassan et al., 2020). Phytochemically, the genus is particularly well known for its triterpenoid saponins, with more than thirty distinct triterpenoid compounds reported across D. inermis and related species, in addition to phenolics, flavonoids, and alkaloids (Liebold et al., 2011; Skała & Szopa, 2023).
Within the Kashmir Valley, where D. inermis grows naturally, Wani, Singh and Pal (2017) reported in vitro phytochemical and antimicrobial activity of the species, providing early regional evidence of its antibacterial potential. More recently, Drakhshaan et al. (2026) conducted a detailed phytochemical and computational evaluation of dichloromethane (DCM) and methanolic (MeOH) extracts of D. inermis, reporting measurable zones of inhibition against Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus subtilis, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Salmonella typhi, and Enterobacter aerogenes using the agar well diffusion technique, with the DCM extract showing the strongest activity overall. However, this and most other antibacterial evaluations of D. inermis have relied on non-polar or semi-polar organic solvents (dichloromethane, methanol, ethanol) capable of extracting terpenoids and steroids, while the antibacterial potential of a simple aqueous extract — the form most relevant to traditional preparation as a decoction or infusion — remains comparatively unexplored. Furthermore, to the best of available literature, the antibacterial efficacy of D. inermis extracts against Klebsiella pneumoniae, an organism of major clinical importance, has not been specifically reported.
This gap forms the rationale for the present study, which evaluates the in vitro antibacterial efficacy of an aqueous extract of D. inermis against E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and K. pneumoniae using the agar well diffusion technique, with the assessment restricted to the zone of inhibition (ZOI) produced against each organism.
1.1 Botanical Description and Taxonomy of Dipsacus inermis
Dipsacus inermis Wall. (common names: Himalayan teasel; local names vary across the Himalayan region) is a robust, erect, perennial herb typically reaching between 1 and 2 metres in height at maturity. The plant produces a stout, ridged, and often sparsely prickly stem arising from a thickened taproot, and is characterised by opposite, sessile to sub-sessile leaves that are lanceolate to elliptic in outline, with margins ranging from entire to coarsely serrate; the leaf bases are frequently connate (fused around the stem), a feature typical of the genus that allows rainwater to collect at the leaf axils. The inflorescence is a dense, solitary, ovoid-to-globose flower head (capitulum) borne terminally on a long, leafless, longitudinally channelled peduncle. Each capitulum is subtended by an involucre of narrow, spine-tipped bracts, and the numerous small, tubular florets within the head are individually subtended by stiff, pointed paleae — a receptacle architecture characteristic of the traditional family Dipsacaceae. Flowering occurs between June and September, with the small florets opening in a characteristic band that progresses from the middle of the head outward in both directions. The fruit is a dry, indehiscent achene enclosed within a persistent epicalyx.
Taxonomically, Dipsacus has historically been placed in its own family, Dipsacaceae; however, molecular phylogenetic studies underpinning the Angiosperm Phylogeny Group (APG) classification system have since subsumed Dipsacaceae within a broadly circumscribed family Caprifoliaceae sensu lato, alongside Valerianaceae and other formerly segregate families (Skała & Szopa, 2023). D. inermis is accordingly classified today under Caprifoliaceae, genus Dipsacus, which comprises approximately 20 recognised species distributed across China, Europe, tropical Africa, and East Asia, with several species, including D. inermis, occurring naturally across the Himalayan belt at moderate to high elevations. Within Kashmir, the species is commonly encountered on open hill slopes, forest margins, and disturbed ground, and has long featured in regional folk medicine.
1.2 Mechanisms of Antibacterial Action of Plant Phytochemicals
The antibacterial activity of crude plant extracts is rarely attributable to a single compound; rather, it typically reflects the combined and often synergistic action of multiple co-occurring phytochemical classes, each capable of interfering with bacterial viability through a distinct mechanism (Cowan, 1999; Jubair et al., 2021). Phenolic compounds and flavonoids are thought to act principally by binding to and disrupting the integrity of the bacterial cytoplasmic membrane, altering its permeability and causing leakage of intracellular contents; several flavonoids have additionally been reported to inhibit bacterial efflux pumps and to interfere with biofilm formation and quorum-sensing signalling, thereby also potentiating the activity of co-administered antibiotics. Tannins act largely through the complexation of proteins, including bacterial enzymes and cell-surface adhesins, reducing enzymatic activity and nutrient availability to the organism, and may additionally chelate essential metal ions required for bacterial metabolism. Terpenoids and steroidal constituents, being predominantly lipophilic, are believed to partition into and disorganise the lipid bilayer of the bacterial membrane, compromising its structural integrity and function; this mechanism is of particular relevance to D. inermis, whose dichloromethane extract has been shown to be dominated by terpenoid, steroidal, and sesquiterpenoid constituents (Drakhshaan et al., 2026). Alkaloids have been proposed to act by intercalating with bacterial DNA or by disrupting membrane-associated processes, while saponins, through their amphipathic, cholesterol-interacting structure, can form pores in the bacterial membrane in a manner analogous to their haemolytic activity. Quinones, for their part, are able to complex irreversibly with nucleophilic amino acid residues in membrane and cell-wall proteins, inactivating them and, in some cases, generating additional oxidative stress within the bacterial cell. Because aqueous extraction preferentially recovers polar constituents — chiefly phenolics, flavonoids, tannins, and certain glycosides — while extracting non-polar terpenoids and steroids far less efficiently than organic solvents such as dichloromethane, the antibacterial profile expected of an aqueous extract of D. inermis would, on mechanistic grounds, be expected to differ from that previously reported for its organic solvent extracts, providing further motivation for the present study.
1.3 CURRENT GAPS IN RESEARCH
Despite the accumulating evidence for the antimicrobial potential of Dipsacus inermis, several specific gaps remain in the published literature. First, essentially all antibacterial evaluations of D. inermis to date, including the most comprehensive recent study (Drakhshaan et al., 2026), have been conducted using dichloromethane or methanolic extracts; no study has specifically isolated and reported the antibacterial efficacy of a simple aqueous extract — the extraction form most consistent with the plant's traditional preparation as a decoction or infusion. Second, no published study, to the best of available literature, has evaluated the antibacterial activity of D. inermis, in any extract form, against Klebsiella pneumoniae, despite this organism's recognised clinical importance and its designation as a critical-priority pathogen in several antimicrobial resistance frameworks. Third, regional evaluation of D. inermis collected from Kashmir remains limited to the relatively early work of Wani, Singh and Pal (2017), with no more recent regional study directly comparing aqueous extract efficacy against clinically relevant Gram-negative organisms using a standardised technique such as agar well diffusion. The present study addresses these three specific gaps.

2. Review of Literature
2.1 Antimicrobial Resistance and the Rationale for Plant-Based Antibacterials
The global burden of bacterial antimicrobial resistance has been comprehensively documented by the Antimicrobial Resistance Collaborators (2022), whose systematic analysis estimated that bacterial AMR was directly responsible for over a million deaths and was associated with several million more worldwide in a single year, with Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa among the leading pathogens contributing to this burden. This has intensified interest in alternative antibacterial strategies, including plant-derived compounds. Cowan (1999), in a widely cited review, catalogued the diversity of plant secondary metabolites — phenolics, quinones, flavonoids, tannins, coumarins, terpenoids, alkaloids, and lectins — that display antimicrobial activity, noting that such compounds often act through multiple, non-specific mechanisms that may reduce the likelihood of resistance development compared with single-target synthetic antibiotics. More recently, Jubair et al. (2021) reviewed the mechanisms by which plant-derived compounds act against multidrug-resistant bacteria, including membrane disruption, efflux pump inhibition, quorum-sensing interference, and biofilm inhibition, reinforcing the rationale for continued screening of medicinal plants against clinically relevant, resistant bacterial strains.
2.2 Mechanisms of Antibacterial Action of Phytochemicals
A substantial body of pharmacognostic literature has characterised the mechanisms by which plant secondary metabolites exert antibacterial effects, and these mechanisms provide the conceptual framework within which the present study's findings must be interpreted (Cowan, 1999; Jubair et al., 2021).
2.2.1 Phenolics and Flavonoids
Phenolic acids and flavonoids are widely reported to disrupt the bacterial cytoplasmic membrane, increasing its permeability and causing leakage of intracellular ions, metabolites, and nucleic acids. Several flavonoids additionally inhibit bacterial efflux pump systems and interfere with quorum-sensing and biofilm formation, mechanisms that are particularly relevant against biofilm-forming organisms such as P. aeruginosa and K. pneumoniae.
2.2.2 Tannins
Tannins act principally through the complexation of proteins, including extracellular bacterial enzymes and surface adhesins, thereby reducing enzymatic activity and impairing bacterial adhesion; they are also capable of chelating metal ions essential for bacterial metabolic processes, indirectly starving the organism of cofactors required for growth.
2.2.3 Terpenoids and Steroids
Terpenoids and steroidal compounds are predominantly lipophilic and are thought to partition into the bacterial lipid bilayer, disorganising membrane structure and compromising its barrier function. This class of compounds was found to dominate the dichloromethane extract of D. inermis in the most detailed phytochemical evaluation of the species to date (Drakhshaan et al., 2026), and is considered a principal contributor to its reported antibacterial activity.
2.2.4 Alkaloids, Saponins, and Quinones
Alkaloids are proposed to act by intercalating with bacterial nucleic acids or by disrupting membrane-associated transport processes. Saponins, owing to their amphipathic structure and affinity for membrane sterols and sterol-like lipids, can form pores in bacterial membranes in a manner analogous to their well-documented haemolytic activity. Quinones can form irreversible complexes with nucleophilic amino acid residues in membrane and cell-wall-associated proteins, inactivating them, and may additionally generate oxidative stress within the bacterial cell.
Because these compound classes differ considerably in polarity, the solvent used for extraction directly determines which classes — and, by extension, which antibacterial mechanisms — predominate in a given plant extract. This principle underlies the comparative discussion, in Chapter 5, between the aqueous extract evaluated in the present study and the organic solvent extracts of D. inermis previously reported in the literature.
2.3 The Genus Dipsacus: Phytochemistry and Biological Activity
The genus Dipsacus (family Caprifoliaceae sensu lato) has attracted sustained phytochemical interest owing to its diverse array of triterpenoid saponins, iridoid glycosides, and phenolic constituents. Zhao and Shi (2011) reviewed the phytochemistry and biological activities of Dipsacus species broadly, highlighting their anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial potential. Skała and Szopa (2023), in a more recent review of Dipsacus and Scabiosa species, catalogued numerous specialised metabolites of high biological relevance across the genus and emphasised the continued pharmacological interest in these plants. Liebold, Straubinger and Rauwald (2011) demonstrated that lipophilic extracts from the roots of the related species Dipsacus sylvestris inhibited the growth of Borrelia burgdorferi, the causative agent of Lyme disease, in vitro, providing early mechanistic support for the antimicrobial reputation of the genus in traditional medicine. Oszmiański (2020) similarly reported notable biological activity in root and leaf extracts of Dipsacus fullonum.
Specific to Dipsacus inermis, Hassan et al. (2020) demonstrated that the species modulates inflammation by inhibiting the NF-κB signalling pathway in both in vitro and in vivo models, supporting its traditional use as an anti-inflammatory agent. Most directly relevant to the present study, Drakhshaan et al. (2026) undertook an integrated phytochemical, in vitro, and computational investigation of D. inermis, evaluating dichloromethane (DCM) and methanol (MeOH) extracts of the whole plant against six bacterial strains — Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus subtilis, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Salmonella typhi, and Enterobacter aerogenes — using the agar well diffusion (zone of inhibition) and broth microdilution (minimum inhibitory concentration) methods. The DCM extract produced the largest zones of inhibition, including 17.87 ± 0.23 mm against S. aureus and E. aerogenes, 16.83 ± 0.29 mm against P. aeruginosa, and 15.93 ± 0.11 mm against E. coli, at a concentration of 100 µg/mL, with a clear dose-dependent reduction in activity at 50 µg/mL, and corresponding minimum inhibitory concentrations as low as 1.562 mg/mL against B. subtilis and E. aerogenes.
GC-MS analysis of the DCM extract by Drakhshaan et al. (2026) identified 85 distinct compounds, with the chemical profile dominated by terpenoids, monoterpenoids, steroids, sesquiterpenoids, sterols, and cannabinoids (collectively 32.94% of the profile), fatty acids such as dodecenedioic acid (18.82%), cyclohexanols and alkanes (8.23%), a phorbol ester (9.41%), and alkaloids (4.70%), alongside minor epoxide and isohumolone constituents. HPLC-UV/Vis analysis of the MeOH extract, in contrast, identified and quantified phenolic and flavonoid constituents, with the highest concentrations recorded for rutin (68.66 µg/mg), reserpine (53.76 µg/mg), and ferulic acid (31.64 µg/mg); quercetin was below the limit of detection. Molecular docking of the identified compounds against five bacterial protein targets — DNA gyrase B, tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase, PBP2X, PBP4, and dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) — identified two terpenoid/alkaloid compounds, designated DI10 and DI31, as potent multi-target leads, and a third, DI22, as selectively active against PBP4; these predictions were further supported by ADMET profiling indicating favourable absorption and low toxicity risk, density functional theory (DFT) and molecular electrostatic potential (MESP) analyses of reactive binding sites, and molecular dynamics simulations demonstrating stable protein–ligand complexes with favourable binding free energies (approximately −45 to −52 kcal/mol by MM-PBSA). Taken together, this study establishes D. inermis as a rich source of antibacterial phytoconstituents, with its activity attributed principally to terpenoid, steroidal, and phenolic compounds recovered by organic solvent extraction; however, the study is explicitly limited to non-polar and semi-polar (DCM, MeOH) extracts, and neither the antibacterial efficacy of a purely aqueous extract nor activity against K. pneumoniae specifically was addressed — the two gaps that the present study is designed to address.
2.4 Dipsacus inermis in the Kashmir Region
Wani, Singh and Pal (2017) carried out an in vitro phytochemical and antimicrobial evaluation of D. inermis collected from the Kashmir Valley, reporting the presence of antimicrobial activity alongside various phytochemical constituents in the species as it occurs regionally. This work provides an important regional precedent for the present study, which similarly sources plant material from the Kashmir region, and highlights the continued relevance of locally available D. inermis as a subject for antibacterial screening.
2.5 Biology and Clinical Significance of the Test Organisms
2.5.1 Escherichia coli
Escherichia coli is a Gram-negative, rod-shaped, facultatively anaerobic bacterium of the family Enterobacteriaceae, first described by Theodor Escherich in 1885. While most strains exist as normal commensal flora of the human and animal gastrointestinal tract, pathogenic strains are classified into distinct pathotypes — including uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC), enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC), enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC), and enterohaemorrhagic E. coli (EHEC) — on the basis of the virulence factors they carry, such as adhesins, toxins, and iron-acquisition systems. UPEC strains in particular are the leading cause of community-acquired urinary tract infections worldwide, while other pathotypes are implicated in gastroenteritis, neonatal meningitis, and bloodstream infections. The increasing global prevalence of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing and carbapenem-resistant E. coli strains has made it a priority organism for antimicrobial susceptibility surveillance and for the screening of novel antibacterial agents.
2.5.2 Pseudomonas aeruginosa
Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a Gram-negative, aerobic, rod-shaped, motile opportunistic pathogen belonging to the family Pseudomonadaceae, frequently identified in clinical settings by its characteristic blue-green pigmentation (pyocyanin) and grape-like odour on culture. It is a major cause of infections in immunocompromised patients, individuals with cystic fibrosis, burn wound patients, and those on mechanical ventilation, and is a leading cause of ventilator-associated pneumonia and catheter-associated infections in intensive care settings. P. aeruginosa is intrinsically resistant to many antibiotic classes owing to its low outer-membrane permeability, constitutively expressed and inducible multidrug efflux pumps, chromosomally encoded AmpC beta-lactamase, and pronounced capacity for biofilm formation, which further shields it from both host immune defences and antimicrobial agents. These features make it one of the more difficult Gram-negative pathogens to treat and a priority target organism for the screening of novel antibacterial agents, including plant-derived compounds.
2.5.3 Klebsiella pneumoniae
Klebsiella pneumoniae is a Gram-negative, encapsulated, non-motile, rod-shaped bacterium of the family Enterobacteriaceae. Its prominent polysaccharide capsule is a key virulence determinant, conferring resistance to phagocytosis and complement-mediated killing, and contributing to the mucoid colony morphology characteristic of the organism on culture media. K. pneumoniae is a significant cause of hospital-acquired pneumonia, urinary tract infections, liver abscesses, wound infections, and bloodstream infections, particularly in immunocompromised, critically ill, and hospitalised patients. The emergence and global spread of carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP), often mediated by carbapenemase enzymes such as KPC and NDM, has been designated a critical-priority pathogen by international public health authorities, as such strains are frequently resistant to nearly all available antibiotic classes, underscoring the urgency of identifying alternative antibacterial agents, including those derived from medicinal plants, that remain effective against this organism.
2.6 The Agar Well Diffusion and Disc Diffusion Techniques
The agar well diffusion method is a widely used, simple, and reproducible technique for the preliminary screening of the antibacterial activity of plant extracts and other test substances. In this method, a bacterial lawn of the test organism is prepared on a solidified agar medium, wells are bored into the agar using a sterile borer, and the test substance is introduced into the wells at a defined volume and concentration. On incubation, the substance diffuses radially into the surrounding medium, and a zone of inhibition — a clear area free of bacterial growth — forms around the well if the substance possesses antibacterial activity. The diameter of this zone, typically measured in millimetres, provides a semi-quantitative measure of antibacterial potency and allows comparison between different extracts, concentrations, and standard antibiotic controls (CLSI, 2024). This method was employed in the present study, following the general approach used for D. inermis extracts by Drakhshaan et al. (2026), to allow direct comparability with previously reported zone of inhibition values for this species.
The disc diffusion method (Kirby-Bauer method) is the related, CLSI-standardised technique conventionally used for antibiotic susceptibility testing, in which a paper disc pre-impregnated with a fixed concentration of a standard antibiotic is placed directly on the surface of the inoculated agar, rather than introduced into a bored well. The antibiotic diffuses outward from the disc, and the resulting zone of inhibition is measured in the same manner as for the well diffusion method. In the present study, the two aqueous extract concentrations (C1, C2) and the negative control were tested by the agar well diffusion technique, while the standard antibiotic (Gentamicin) positive control was tested by the disc diffusion technique — the conventional method for antibiotic controls — with both sets of zones read from the same inoculated plates for direct comparability (CLSI, 2024).
2.7 Research Gap and Rationale
Taken together, the reviewed literature establishes that D. inermis possesses antibacterial activity when extracted with organic solvents such as dichloromethane and methanol, and that the species has a history of traditional and regional use, including within Kashmir. However, two specific gaps remain: first, the antibacterial efficacy of a simple aqueous extract of D. inermis — the extraction form most consistent with traditional preparation — has not been systematically evaluated using the agar well diffusion technique; and second, its activity against Klebsiella pneumoniae, a clinically critical Gram-negative pathogen, has not been specifically reported. The present study addresses both gaps.

3.2.2 Processing and Shade Drying
After collection, the plant material was thoroughly washed with distilled water to remove adhering soil and debris, and then shade-dried at room temperature for several days to preserve heat-labile phytochemical constituents (Fig. 1). The shade-dried material was subsequently pulverised into a coarse powder using a mortar and pestle (Fig. 2, Fig. 3), and the powdered material was stored in an airtight container at room temperature, protected from light and moisture, until further use.

3.2.3 Preparation of Aqueous Extract
The aqueous extract of D. inermis was prepared using the Soxhlet (hot continuous) extraction technique (Fig. 4, Fig. 5). Briefly, 50 g of the coarsely powdered plant material was loaded into a thimble and extracted with 500 mL of distilled water (1:10, w/v) using a round-bottom flask fitted with a reflux condenser mounted on a heating mantle, run continuously for 24 hours. The resulting extract was filtered, first through muslin cloth and then through Whatman No. 1 filter paper, to remove particulate matter, and was subsequently concentrated and stored in vials at 4C. The yield of the concentrated aqueous extract was recorded as 24.13% (w/w, relative to the starting powdered plant material). The concentrated extract was reconstituted in sterile distilled water to obtain two working concentrations for the antibacterial assay — C1 (50 µg) and C2 (100 µg) — matching the concentration range used by Drakhshaan et al. (2026) for comparability.

3.2.5 Antibacterial Susceptibility Testing: Agar Well Diffusion and Disc Diffusion Assays
The antibacterial activity of the aqueous extract of D. inermis was evaluated using two complementary diffusion techniques on the same inoculated plates: the agar well diffusion technique for the two extract concentrations and the negative control, and the disc diffusion technique for the standard antibiotic positive control. This combination follows the general procedure described for D. inermis extracts by Drakhshaan et al. (2026), with modifications. Sterile [insert medium used, e.g., Mueller-Hinton agar / LB agar] was poured into sterile Petri dishes and allowed to solidify under aseptic conditions. The standardised bacterial suspension of each test organism was uniformly spread over the surface of the solidified agar to form a confluent lawn.
Agar Well Diffusion (Extract Concentrations and Negative Control)
Wells of approximately 6 mm diameter were bored into the inoculated agar using a sterile cork/gel borer. A volume of [insert volume introduced per well, e.g., 50–100 µL, at each of the C1 (50 µg) and C2 (100 µg) concentrations] of the aqueous extract was introduced into separate wells for the C1 and C2 concentrations, while [insert negative control used, e.g., sterile distilled water] was introduced into a further well to serve as the negative control.
Disc Diffusion (Positive Control)
A sterile, commercially prepared Gentamicin antibiotic disc was placed directly on the surface of the same inoculated agar to serve as the positive control, following the conventional disc diffusion (Kirby-Bauer) method used for antibiotic susceptibility testing (CLSI, 2024), rather than being introduced into a bored well. Placing the positive control disc alongside the extract-loaded wells on the same plate allowed the zone of inhibition produced by the extract to be directly benchmarked against that of a standard antibiotic under identical incubation conditions.
The plates prepared by both methods were left undisturbed for a short period to allow diffusion of the test substances into the agar, and were then incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. Each test — for both the well diffusion and disc diffusion components — was performed in triplicate.
3.2.6 Measurement and Interpretation of Zone of Inhibition
Following incubation, the diameter of the clear zone of inhibition (ZOI) surrounding each well or disc — including the diameter of the well or disc itself — was measured in millimetres (mm) using a transparent ruler. The mean of the triplicate measurements was calculated for each organism and each treatment. As no CLSI interpretive breakpoints exist for plant extracts, the zone of inhibition values obtained by well diffusion for the aqueous extract were interpreted descriptively and compared with the disc diffusion zone obtained for the Gentamicin positive control, the negative control, and previously reported values for organic solvent extracts of D. inermis (Drakhshaan et al., 2026).

5.1 Extraction Yield in Context
The 24.13% (w/w) yield obtained for the aqueous extract in the present study is substantially higher than the 2.72% and 2.9% yields reported by Drakhshaan et al. (2026) for the dichloromethane and methanolic extracts of D. inermis, respectively, prepared from the same starting material. This is consistent with the general behaviour of water as an extraction solvent: being highly polar and miscible with a broad range of plant constituents, water tends to co-extract not only phenolics, flavonoids, tannins, and glycosides but also bulk polar material such as soluble sugars, mucilage, and polysaccharides, resulting in a higher overall crude yield than more selective organic solvents such as dichloromethane. A higher crude yield does not, however, imply a proportionally higher concentration of antibacterially active constituents — indeed, it may reflect a greater proportion of inert or non-bioactive polar material — and this distinction is relevant to interpreting the zone of inhibition results that follow.

5.4 Possible Phytochemical Basis for the Observed Activity
The phytochemical profile reported for D. inermis provides a plausible mechanistic basis for interpreting the present findings. Drakhshaan et al. (2026) found the antibacterial dichloromethane extract to be dominated by terpenoids, steroids, sesquiterpenoids, and fatty acids (identified by GC-MS), while the methanolic extract was characterised chiefly by phenolic and flavonoid constituents — including rutin, reserpine, and ferulic acid — identified by HPLC. Aqueous extraction, being a highly polar process, is inherently far less efficient than dichloromethane at solubilising non-polar terpenoids and steroids, but is well suited to recovering polar phenolics, flavonoids, tannins, and glycosides. On this basis, any antibacterial activity produced by the aqueous extract in the present study would be mechanistically attributable chiefly to these polar constituents, acting principally through membrane permeabilisation and protein/enzyme complexation rather than through the membrane-lipid disruption associated with terpenoids and steroids (Cowan, 1999; Jubair et al., 2021; see also Section 2.2). Conversely, if the aqueous extract showed markedly weaker activity than the organic solvent extracts reported previously, this would itself be consistent with the terpenoid- and steroid-dominated profile of the more active DCM extract, and would support the inference that these non-polar constituents are the principal drivers of D. inermis's antibacterial potential rather than its polar phytochemical fraction.

The present study evaluated the in vitro antibacterial efficacy of an aqueous extract of Dipsacus inermis against three clinically important Gram-negative bacterial strains — Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Klebsiella pneumoniae — using the agar well diffusion technique, with efficacy assessed in terms of the zone of inhibition. While the present in vitro findings provide preliminary evidence regarding the antibacterial potential of the aqueous extract of D. inermis, further studies are recommended, including phytochemical screening and quantification of the aqueous extract itself, determination of the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) against the tested organisms, evaluation of a wider range of extract concentrations, and eventual in vivo validation, before any therapeutic application can be considered.
In conclusion, this study contributes to the limited body of literature on the aqueous extract of D. inermis and reinforces the broader case for continued screening of regionally available medicinal plants of the Kashmir Himalaya as potential sources of plant-derived antibacterial agents against clinically significant, and increasingly resistant, Gram-negative pathogens.
