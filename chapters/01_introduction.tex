\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is the capacity of a bacterium to grow in the presence of a drug that once suppressed it. Gram-negative rods are a large part of that problem because their outer membrane, built from lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and porin proteins, slows the entry of many antibiotics \citep{nikaido2003molecular,pages2008porin}. Pumps of the resistance--nodulation--division (RND) family then remove drug that does enter \citep{poole2005efflux}. \taxa{Escherichia coli}, \taxa{Pseudomonas aeruginosa}, and \taxa{Klebsiella pneumoniae} are listed among current priority pathogens for that reason \citep{amrcollaborators2022global,who2024bacterial}. They cause urinary tract infection, pneumonia, wound infection, and bacteraemia in hospitals and in the community.

The 2019 global burden analysis linked bacterial AMR to a large share of infectious deaths, with these three organisms among the leading contributors \citep{amrcollaborators2022global}. The World Health Organization bacterial priority list keeps carbapenem-resistant \taxa{Acinetobacter}, Enterobacterales, and \taxa{P. aeruginosa} in the critical group \citep{who2024bacterial}. Plant secondary metabolites remain a source of compounds that act on membranes and proteins rather than on a single enzyme \citep{cowan1999plant,jubair2021review}.

Water extracts are not equivalent to dichloromethane or methanol extracts. Water favours phenolics, tannins, flavonoids, saponins, and glycosides. Non-polar solvents favour terpenoids and steroids \citep{cowan1999plant,azwanida2015review}. Traditional decoctions are aqueous. An \invitro{} screen of a water extract therefore answers a different question from a screen of an organic extract of the same plant.

\section{Gram-negative test organisms}

\taxa{Escherichia coli} is a facultative rod of the Enterobacterales. Commensal strains occupy the gut. Pathogenic lineages cause urinary infection, diarrhoea, neonatal meningitis, and bloodstream infection. Extended-spectrum beta-lactamases and carbapenemases are now common in clinical collections \citep{amrcollaborators2022global}.

\taxa{Pseudomonas aeruginosa} is an aerobic opportunistic rod. It produces pigments, forms biofilms, and expresses inducible AmpC, low outer-membrane permeability, and several RND pumps \citep{hancock2000antibiotic,poole2005efflux}. Burn wounds, ventilated lungs, and catheters are typical sites.

\taxa{Klebsiella pneumoniae} is a capsulated, non-motile enterobacterium. The capsule resists phagocytosis. Hospital pneumonia, urinary infection, liver abscess, and bacteraemia are typical presentations. Carbapenemase-producing lineages are treated as a critical-priority group \citep{who2024bacterial}. The three organisms therefore share an outer-membrane barrier but differ in pump repertoire and capsule, which is why they were tested in parallel.

\section{The study plant}

\taxa{Dipsacus inermis} Wall.\ is a perennial herb of the genus \textit{Dipsacus}. Older floras placed the genus in Dipsacaceae. The Angiosperm Phylogeny Group treatment folds that family into Caprifoliaceae \textit{sensu lato} \citep{apgiv2016,skala2023dipsacus}. The plant is recognised by opposite leaves, a ridged stem, and a solitary globose head of florets on a long peduncle. Flowering in the western Himalaya falls in the summer monsoon months. Local Kashmiri names include wopal haakh \citep{skala2023dipsacus,lone2015study}.

The genus is rich in triterpenoid saponins, iridoid glycosides, and phenolics \citep{zhao2011phytochemicals,skala2023dipsacus}. Lipophilic root extracts of the related \taxa{Dipsacus sylvestris} inhibit \taxa{Borrelia burgdorferi} \invitro{} \citep{liebold2011growth}. Leaf and root extracts of \taxa{Dipsacus fullonum} also show biological activity \citep{oszmianski2020roots}. For \taxa{D. inermis} itself, a Kashmir group reported NF-$\kappa$B-dependent anti-inflammatory activity \invitro{} and \invivo{} \citep{hassan2020dipsacus}. Dichloromethane and methanol extracts of the species inhibit several bacteria in well-diffusion and broth-dilution assays, with the dichloromethane fraction the more active of the two \citep{drakhshaan2026identification}. Those antibacterial data do not include an aqueous Soxhlet extract, and they do not include \taxa{K. pneumoniae}.

\section{Rationale}

The experimental gap is limited. Aqueous Soxhlet extraction is the form closest to a decoction. The antibacterial procedure in this study is limited to a diffusion assay on seeded agar, scored only as the zone of inhibition. Gentamicin on a paper disc (GEN 5, \SI{5}{\micro\gram}) is the positive control. Distilled water is the negative control. The work was done with plant material from the Kashmir University Botanical Garden and with bacterial stocks held in the Advanced Research Laboratory, Department of Zoology.

\section{Objectives of the study}\label{sec:objectives}

The study was carried out with the following objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item To collect \taxa{Dipsacus inermis} from the Kashmir University Botanical Garden, obtain botanical authentication, and prepare an aqueous extract by Soxhlet extraction followed by concentration.
  \item To record the preliminary qualitative phytochemical profile of that aqueous extract across the major secondary-metabolite classes that the tube tests can detect.
  \item To measure the \invitro{} antibacterial effect of the extract at two working concentrations against \taxa{Escherichia coli}, \taxa{Pseudomonas aeruginosa}, and \taxa{Klebsiella pneumoniae} sourced from the Advanced Research Laboratory, using disc diffusion for gentamicin GEN 5, distilled water as the negative control, and zone of inhibition as the sole measurement.
\end{enumerate}
