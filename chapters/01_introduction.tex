\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a major clinical and public-health problem \citep{amrcollaborators2022,who2024}. Antibiotics still underpin surgery, chemotherapy, and the treatment of common bacterial infections, but overuse in clinics, households, and livestock has selected multidrug-resistant lineages. The Global Research on Antimicrobial Resistance analysis in \textit{The Lancet} associated bacterial AMR with a large share of deaths worldwide in 2019 \citep{amrcollaborators2022}. Gram-negative rods are particularly difficult to inhibit: an outer membrane rich in lipopolysaccharide, periplasmic enzymes, and efflux pumps restrict many drugs and many plant phenolics \citep{nikaido2003,li2015,blair2015}. \textit{Escherichia coli}, \textit{Pseudomonas aeruginosa}, and \textit{Klebsiella pneumoniae} account for a large share of community and hospital Gram-negative disease and sit, in carbapenem-resistant form, in the critical tier of the WHO Bacterial Priority Pathogens List \citep{who2024,sati2025}.

New synthetic antibiotic classes that work against Gram-negative bacteria remain scarce, so plant extracts continue to be screened \citep{cowan1999,jubair2021}. Vascular plants make phenolics, flavonoids, tannins, terpenoids, saponins, alkaloids, and related glycosides as part of their defence chemistry \citep{cowan1999,cushnie2005,kumar2013}. Those mixed metabolites are often described as acting on more than one cellular process. Such statements are literature generalisations; they were not measured on the aqueous extract used here.

The western Himalaya, including the Kashmir Valley, holds many plants used as decoctions or poultices. Most of those plants still lack simple, documented in vitro antibacterial measurements on aqueous preparations. This dissertation tests one of them, \textit{Dipsacus inermis} Wall.\ ex DC.\ (Himalayan teasel; Caprifoliaceae sensu lato), as an aqueous extract \citep{apgiv2016,skala2023}. The genus \textit{Dipsacus} is known for triterpenoid saponins, iridoid glycosides, and phenolics \citep{zhao2011,skala2023,oszmianski2020}. Kashmir material of \textit{D.\ inermis} has been studied for anti-inflammatory activity \citep{hassan2020}. Dichloromethane and methanol extracts of the same species from northern Pakistan have published antibacterial data against several bacteria, including \textit{E.\ coli} and \textit{P.\ aeruginosa}, but not \textit{K.\ pneumoniae} \citep{drakhshaan2026}. An aqueous extract tested by disc diffusion against laboratory stocks of those three Gram-negative organisms therefore remains a practical gap.

The work was done at the Advanced Research Laboratory, Department of Zoology, University of Kashmir. Its aims were three. First, to collect, identify, and prepare the aqueous extract of \textit{Dipsacus inermis}. Second, to evaluate the preliminary phytochemical profile of that aqueous extract. Third, to evaluate the antibacterial activity of the extract against clinical pathogenic bacterial strains of \textit{E.\ coli}, \textit{P.\ aeruginosa}, and \textit{K.\ pneumoniae}. The chapters that follow set out the literature (Ch.\ 3), the laboratory protocol (Ch.\ 4), the findings (Ch.\ 5), and their limits (Ch.\ 6--7). The numbered statement of the same three aims is given in Chapter~2, as required by the departmental sequence, and is not repeated at length elsewhere.
